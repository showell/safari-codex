================================================================================
A. DEFECTS AND HOLES
================================================================================

    Things that are wrong, or missing, in the toolchain.

A1. real-to-int and real-from-int have no zig emitter.        [HOLE - FIXED]

    "error: zig plug: no emitter for real-to-int" (and real-from-int). Both
    confirmed missing independently.

    Consequences, in order of how much they cost:
      - Gpu chapter DeviceMath is PARTLY dark: real-sin and real-cos are, because
        dm-reduce calls real-from-int. The port carries port/Trig.codex as a
        stand-in for exactly that.
        CORRECTED 2026-08-29, and the correction is worth having. This bullet
        used to read "DeviceMath CANNOT BE TRANSPILED AT ALL", which is wrong,
        because emission is per-function and not per-chapter (B6). real-sqrt
        does NOT call dm-reduce -- it is a scaled Heron iteration and touches no
        conversion at all -- so a chapter that cites DeviceMath and reaches only
        real-sqrt transpiles, builds and runs TODAY. Measured by doing it:
        port/GuardRail.codex cites Gpu chapter DeviceMath for its post-direction
        square root and is graded green against the zig. The stand-in's footprint
        is therefore smaller than this file claimed: Trig owes upstream sine and
        cosine, and nothing else.
      - No counted loop can widen its index. Every `i / n * two-pi` becomes a
        Real accumulator threaded alongside the Integer counter. Three sites in
        the port so far (Tree's cone ring, Scene's ridge, Scene's canopy).
      - Combined with A2 below, a transpiled program CANNOT REPORT A COMPUTED
        REAL AT ALL. That is the expensive one — see the note under A2.

    The fix looks small: each is one ZigBuiltinEmitter table row plus one prelude
    helper, the shape of bits-to-real-approx at ZigEmitter.codex:1036 and :3784.
    Bare metal is cvttsd2si / cvtsi2sd (X86_64Builtins.codex:1663-1679) —
    truncate toward zero, INT64_MIN on NaN or overflow. Zig's @intFromFloat
    truncates the same way but is UB out of range, so the honest emission is a
    guarded prelude helper, not a bare cast.

    CLOSED 2026-08-29. It was that small, and the estimate above was accurate.
    cobblestone-realconv a2a2f6f4, "zig plug: emit real-to-int and real-from-int".
    Two table rows, two prelude parts, two reserved names. The guards match the
    hardware over 31 values checked by inline asm — zero disagreements — and the
    transpiler's fixed point holds byte-identically across the change. Full
    account in PLUG_WORK.md; upstream row is plugs-backlog 2.03. NOT YET SENT.

    The two counted-loop consequences are retired with it: a Real accumulator no
    longer has to be threaded beside an Integer counter, and Tree's cone ring,
    Scene's ridge and Scene's canopy could each drop theirs. They have NOT been
    rewritten — the ports are graded green as they stand and there is no reason
    to churn them, but a new module need not pay that any more.

    AND DEVICEMATH IS NOW FULLY CLEAR. The correction above said only real-sin
    and real-cos were dark, via dm-reduce. dm-reduce is exactly what real-to-int
    and real-from-int unblock, so nothing in that chapter is dark any more --
    checked, not assumed: citing it and calling real-sin 100.0 transpiles, builds
    and answers -0.506, which means the reduction over sixteen turns is running.
    port/Trig.codex is therefore RETIRABLE. It has not been retired, and that is
    a hold rather than an oversight: Trig's wrap subtracts in a bounded loop
    where dm-reduce divides and converts back through the integers, so the two
    disagree in the last bits, and four graded chapters cite Trig today. It is
    its own change with its own regrade.

    STILL DARK, and unchanged by this: real-approx-* (the f32 family). Filling
    those would make the house-wide f32-computed-as-f64 collapse observable for
    the first time, which is section 6's conversation and not this one.

A2. `show` on a Real is refused — deliberately, and the refusal is right, but
    the CONSEQUENCE is worth stating.               [BY DESIGN - COST RETIRED]

    @compileError("zig plug: show on a Real needs __real_to_text, which this plug
    does not have"). std.fmt would agree with bare metal on some values and not
    others, so it refuses rather than guess. Correct.

    But with A1 also missing there is NO WAY OUT for a Real: it cannot be
    printed, and it cannot be converted to something that can. A failing test can
    say WHERE it failed and not WHAT it computed.

    Measured cost, twice this session:
      - Calibrating the camera tolerance meant BINARY-SEARCHING it (tighten,
        rebuild, see which seams go red) instead of reading an error.
      - A red coordinate in the tree port had to be diagnosed by re-simulating
        the whole cone in f64 in a separate zig program, to prove that
        20.606868 vs the gold's 20.606842 was width and not a bug.
    Landing A1 retires both: real-to-int (x * 1000.0) gives scaled-integer dumps
    and plain diffs.

    AND IT DID, 2026-08-29. The refusal on `show` still stands and is still
    right — nothing here argues with it — but a Real is no longer trapped:

        scaled : Real -> Integer
        scaled (x) = real-to-int (x * 1000.0)

        scaled 3.14159    ->  3141
        scaled -2.71828   -> -2718

    Grade can grow the scaled-integer dump its own prose anticipated, and the
    tolerance arguments can start reporting what they actually saw. Neither has
    been done yet; the grader is unchanged and every seam still reports an index.

A3. OvError silently becomes a WRAPPING multiply.                    [DEFECT]

    Codex declares int-ty-default : CodexType = IntegerTy i64-min i64-max
    OvError (Types/CodexType.codex:129). The zig plug emits `*%`.

        a * a   where a = 4000000000
        ->  -2446744073709551616      exit 0, no diagnostic

    Exactly the i64 wrap. Same family as the trapping/saturating collapse at
    ZigEmitter.codex:1270-1273. This is a silent wrong answer, and it is the
    single reason a fixed-point port of this game was rejected: its whole
    technique is large intermediate products.
    Repro: price-b/ovf2.codex.

A4. Math chapter Cordic is four times less accurate than it claims, and its
    test never calls it.                                             [DEFECT]

    Docstring: "16 iterations gives ~0.1% accuracy." Measured worst error 0.45%
    (cordic-sin 300 = 291 against a true 295.52). Its atan table
    [785,463,244,124,62,31,15,7,3,1,0,0,0,0,0,0] saturates — the last six
    iterations are no-ops.

    Upstream's gate cannot see it: codex/test/forewords/math-cordic.expected is
    the single line "Math/Cordic OK", and the test body only prints that string.
    Nothing anywhere calls a Cordic function.
    Repro: price-b/cordic_probe.codex.

A5. A multi-line application inside a `let` emits @panic AS A CALLEE, with no
    diagnostic.                                                      [DEFECT]

    This one is worth a careful look, because the same shape is DIAGNOSED
    elsewhere (CDX1070, item B1) and here it is not.

        let pts =
          [ ScreenPt { ... }, ... ]
        in ...

    emitted, for the value of pts:

        b4: { const _f4 = @panic("\x01"); break :b4 _f4.call(_f4.ctx, <the list>); }

    The list was parsed as an ARGUMENT to something the emitter had no value for,
    so it emitted a panic where the callee goes and then called it. Codegen
    reported no error; zig then rejected it as unreachable code, which is the
    only reason it was noticed. Putting the literal on one line fixes it.

    A program that failed this way in an expression zig happened to accept would
    be a runtime panic with no source-level explanation.

A6. Single-letter function names a, b, c, d are unusable.            [DEFECT]

    A Codex function named `d` emits `fn d_`, which collides with the Tup4
    comptime parameter `d_`:
        error: function parameter shadows declaration of 'd_'
    Found by accident naming a one-line helper `d`. Cheap to hit, cheap to fix,
    and a mangling collision rather than anything deep.

A7. Real is f64 in every plug; bare metal computes f32 in f32.       [KNOWN]

    Not found here — recorded in NOTES.txt section 6 and re-confirmed. Listed so
    this file is complete: ZigEmitter.codex:297 and :328 map every RealTy to
    f64 whatever width the type asked for, and 1270-1273 collapse the approx /
    trapping / saturating opcodes onto one operator, so TRAPPING does not trap
    and SATURATING does not saturate. The C# plug is character-for-character
    identical and the wasm plug states it as policy, so this is a house-wide
    convention and NOT a zig quirk — ask before patching. The f32 conversion
    builtins are all missing too, which is why the collapse has never been
    observable from this arm: the hole hides the wrong answer.

================================================================================
B. CORRECT BUT SURPRISING -- read before writing Codex
================================================================================

    The language and the emitter behaving as designed, in ways that cost time.

B1. CDX1070 -- AN APPLICATION MAY NOT SPAN A NEWLINE, AND NEITHER MAY A LIST.
    "Application ended at newline; '[' on line N is not parsed as an argument."
    Wrap the application in parentheses, bind it with a let, or put the arguments
    on one line. See A5 for the case where it is NOT diagnosed.

    THE SAME HOLDS FOR A LIST LITERAL, which is worth saying because the code that
    hits it is usually being formatted for readability -- exactly when you break a
    line. Broken across lines it halts with CDX1025 "Unclosed list literal:
    expected ',' or ']'". Each list is one line, however long: the generated
    EmojiStills bull runs to about 200 KB on a single line, which is fine in a
    file and is a fair argument for generating that kind of chapter rather than
    typing it.

B2. CDX1023 — a `let` nested inside an `if` arm inside a `let` is refused.
    "Expected 'in' after let bindings." Flatten it: hoist the inner computation
    into its own function. Doing so in Geom's clip-near EXPOSED A REAL BUG of
    mine, so the refusal earned its keep.

B3. CDX2085 — `==` between Reals is refused, and CODEX IS RIGHT.
    "Floating-point equality is not safe. Use the ~ operator: x ~ y
    (approximately equal, 4 ULP tolerance), x ~0 y (bitwise exact, if you are
    certain)." zig writes a plain == and says nothing.

    This forced a real decision in tree.zig's hull sort. The zig's `a.x == b.x`
    is bitwise, so the port needs ~0. A 4-ULP ~ would have called distinct ring
    points equal, reordered the sort, and changed the hull's POINT COUNT — a
    structural error that a coordinate tolerance would never have caught.
    Best single argument in this file for Codex over zig.

B4. CDX2001 — no implicit Integer to Real widening.
    `0.0 + i` is a type error, not a widening. With A1 missing there is no
    conversion either, so carry a Real accumulator beside the Integer counter.

B5. CDX3001 — chapters share ONE FLAT NAMESPACE inside a bundle.
    Citing Math chapter Geometry for its convex-hull collided with the port's own
    Geom: "Duplicate type definition: 'Vec3' is already defined", 21 errors.
    Citing a foreword chapter is not free of a port's own type names. The hull
    was ported instead, which is more faithful anyway.

    IT BITES ENTRY CHAPTERS TOO, and there the collision is on a name you did not
    write. A second spike entry cited the first for its printing helpers and got
    "CDX3001 Duplicate definition: opening" — both are entry chapters, both define
    `opening`, and one bundle cannot hold two. The fix is the ordinary one for a
    flat namespace: the shared part becomes its own chapter (poc/SpikePrint) and
    both entries cite that. Worth knowing before writing the second entry, because
    the natural first move — cite the working one — cannot work.

B6. The emitter emits only what the entry can REACH, and silently.
    A first attempt at the scene chapter had an `opening` that referenced nothing
    in it. Result: exit 0, no diagnostic, and a ZERO-BYTE program. If a chapter
    vanishes, check that opening actually touches it.

B7. `Integer between lo and hi clamping` is real and type-enforced.
    From arith.codex: Score { v = 250 } with `v : Integer between 0 and 100
    clamping` yields 100. Worth remembering for colour channels.

B8. Integer division truncates toward zero, both signs.
    -7/2 = -3, 7/-2 = -3. Matches zig @divTrunc. There is no floor division.

B9. B6's reachability rule CUTS BOTH WAYS, and the useful direction is the one
    nobody had tried: a chapter with an unemittable function in it is not itself
    unemittable. B6 records the cost -- an unreached chapter silently vanishes.
    The benefit is the same mechanism: cite a chapter for the functions you CAN
    emit and the ones you cannot are never reached, so they are never emitted and
    never diagnosed.

    This is how the port got a real square root. Gpu chapter DeviceMath was
    written off wholesale (see the correction under A1) on the strength of
    dm-reduce needing real-from-int. But real-sqrt is independent of it:

        real-sqrt (x) = if x <= 0.0 then 0.0 else dm-sqrt-scaled x 1.0 700

    -- a scale by fours into [0.25, 4) and five Newton steps, all in Real. Citing
    DeviceMath and touching only real-sqrt transpiles clean and prints the right
    answers. Took four minutes to test and retired a stand-in that would otherwise
    have grown a third function.

    THE RULE TO CARRY: before writing a stand-in for a foreword chapter, check
    which of ITS functions actually reach the hole. "This chapter is dark" is
    almost never true; "these functions are dark" usually is.

B10. B3's ~0 comes up again, and the second instance is more dangerous than the
    first. guard_rail.zig guards a division by a run length:

        if (len == 0) len = 1;

    Codex refuses the bare == (CDX2085) and the caller must choose. It is ~0,
    bitwise, because the zig's == is. Choosing the 4-ULP ~ instead would fire the
    fallback on path points that are merely CLOSE, swinging that post from its own
    run direction to the fixed one -- a post visibly rotated on screen, not a
    rounding difference. Unlike the hull case in B3 it would not change any point
    count, so the structural half of the judge would not catch it: only the
    coordinate gate would, and only because the error is large.

B11. A CODEX KEYWORD USED AS A LET BINDING IS DIAGNOSED AS A MISSING `in`.

    mountains.zig normalises a fraction it calls `above`. Porting that name
    verbatim halts codegen with

        CDX1023 Expected 'in' after let bindings

    over a `let` that has its `in`, on the line above the real problem. `above` is
    a KEYWORD -- Lexer.codex:333, `else if w == "above" then AboveKeyword`, sitting
    with the proof-language set beside `cites`, `claim`, `proof`, `qed` and
    `induction`. The refusal is right; the message names the wrong construct and
    does not mention the keyword or its position.

    Cost a bisect: no error line is reported anywhere, codexzig writes only the
    HALT summary, so the chapter had to be truncated repeatedly to find which
    definition carried it. Watch for it whenever a zig identifier is copied
    across -- `above` is an ordinary word in graphics code and not an obvious
    keyword. Renaming to `above-frac` is the whole fix.

B12. AN ALIAS-SHAPED DEFINITION IS INLINED AND IS NOT THERE TO CALL.

    A Codex definition whose body is a single application does not become a zig
    function. Six written for the wasm shim's convenience --

        scene-sun-x-at (u) = (scene-sun-at u).x

    -- emitted nothing at all; `report`, their only Codex caller, had
    `scene_sun_at(u_).x` inlined into it, and the wasm build failed on six
    undeclared identifiers.

    This only bites at the boundary, and that is the thing to remember: inside
    Codex an inlined alias is invisible and harmless, but the SHIM IS OUTSIDE THE
    PROGRAM and can call only what survives. Write the shim against the real
    functions -- `sky-color`, `sun-pos` -- rather than against wrappers, and drop
    wrappers that exist purely to be called from zig.

    Note the family resemblance to B6: both are the emitter declining to emit
    something, silently, and both surface as a missing name downstream rather than
    as a diagnostic.

    IT HAPPENED AGAIN WIRING THE REAL RIDER INTO THE BROWSER, which is worth
    recording because the shape was identical and the note did not prevent it.
    Drive grew

        sun-for (w) (s) = sun-pos (heading-for s) (step-for w s) focal camera-w

    -- one application, so inlined, so absent -- and the shim failed on four
    undeclared identifiers. Its neighbours `heading-for` and `step-for` DID emit,
    because both are arithmetic rather than a single call, which is exactly what
    makes the rule easy to trip: two definitions written together in the same
    style, one survives and one does not, and nothing says which.

    The fix is B12's own advice taken literally: the shim calls sun-pos, and the
    wrapper stays for the Codex side to read. Check every name the shim uses with
    `grep -n "^fn <mangled>(" build/scene.zig` BEFORE building the wasm; the error
    otherwise arrives from the zig compiler, three steps downstream.

    A THIRD TIME, AND IT IS NOT ONLY ALIASES: A CONDITIONAL BODY INLINES TOO.
    Porting safari.zig's roll deadband as

        rider-roll (s) = if real-abs s.tilt < roll-deadband then 0.0 else s.tilt

    emitted nothing, and the shim failed on an undeclared `rider_roll`. That is
    not an alias by any reading -- it is a branch.

    MEASURED over four variants written side by side, because guessing the rule
    twice was enough. The same `if` over a SCALAR parameter emits; the same `if`
    over a RECORD parameter does not, whether its arms are a bare field, a field
    times one, or a nested conditional. And the arithmetic-bodied `view-yaw-for`
    beside it, which also takes a record, emits fine. So the trigger is narrower
    than "one expression" and wider than "one application", and the useful
    formulation is: A ONE-EXPRESSION BODY MAY VANISH; BIND SOMETHING WITH A `let`
    AND IT WILL NOT. `let t = s.tilt in if ...` emits, and that is the whole fix.

    The rule to carry is still B12's: grep for the mangled name before building.

B13. A NULLARY BINDING EMITS AS A FUNCTION, NOT A MEMOISED CONSTANT.

    `segs : List Segment` / `segs = build-world` reads like a constant. It emits:

        fn segs() *CxList(Segment) { return build_world(); }

    so every mention REBUILDS all nineteen segments with their trees, herds and
    cat placements. The browser scene's `pos-from` mentions it twice per loop
    iteration, so one frame rebuilt the world hundreds of times.

    Measured: 270 ms a frame, about four per second. Threading the world down as
    a parameter -- which every graded chapter in the port already does, because a
    check has to hand it in anyway -- took the same frame to 25 ms. SIX TIMES,
    for a change that moved no arithmetic and produced byte-identical output.

    The tell is that it is invisible in the Codex. `segs` looks free at every use
    site and the cost is entirely in the emitter's choice. Worth a look at the
    generated zig whenever a nullary binding wraps real work: the fix is always to
    pass it, and passing it is what the port does everywhere else anyway.

    Related to B12 -- both are the emitter treating a definition as a rewrite rule
    rather than a stored thing. B12's alias vanishes; this one duplicates.

================================================================================
C. ENVIRONMENT
================================================================================

    The transpiler, the heap, the harness, and the browser.

C1. codexzig writes the ZIG TO STDERR and diagnostics to stdout.
    ON A HALT IT WRITES THE REASON TO STDERR TOO — so the output file is short
    and NON-EMPTY rather than empty, and a `[ -s ]` test calls that success. This
    cost a confusing failure; harness/build_wasm.sh now greps for the prelude
    marker instead.

C2. A transpiled program prints to STDERR.
    print-line-uni becomes std.debug.print. Every harness here reads stderr.

C3. Codex has no mutable globals.
    camera.zig's `pub var view_w` is threaded as a parameter in the port. It is
    the camera's only mutable state.

C4. Codex has no libm, and its foreword is mostly INTEGER fixed point.
    There are no transcendental builtins at all — only conversions and the
    arithmetic modes. DeviceMath is a 78-line hand-written Codex chapter, and it
    is one of only ten files in the whole foreword that use Real. Cordic,
    Easing, Color, GameCamera and Numeric are all Integer milli-units.

C5. wasm32-freestanding is FOUR SUBSTITUTIONS IN THE FIXED PRELUDE away, not the
    gulf NOTES section 5 describes. They are: the entry, which spawns a thread
    purely to get a 512 MB stack; the two print functions, which reach std.Io and
    drag in posix; the heap reserve constant, which asks for 4 GiB, the whole of a
    wasm32 address space; and cx_heap_base, which wants a page_allocator that does
    not exist there and becomes a static .bss region instead.

    NOTHING IN THE TRANSPILED PROGRAM CHANGES -- only the fixed prelude, which is
    why this is a text pass over four known shapes rather than a fork of the
    emitter. Each substitution must match EXACTLY ONCE or harness/wasmify.py
    refuses, so a prelude that drifts fails loudly instead of silently.

C6. The bump heap never reclaims. Reserved once, bumped forever. Fine for one
    frame; a long-running process rebuilding a scene per frame will exhaust it.
    Design for one probe process per short horizon.

    SIX SPIKE VIEWPOINTS IS WHAT ONE NATIVE PROCESS HOLDS, measured by exceeding
    it. The spike binary prints one still per viewpoint and never rewinds — C8's
    arena reset is a thing the wasm SHIM does, and a native binary has no shim —
    so a run accumulates every frame it has printed. Six came to within 1.8 KB of
    the reserve; a seventh aborted with

        cx heap: exhausted at 4294965457 + 7000 of 4294967296

    at about 700 MB a frame. The fix is a second process, not a bigger reserve: a
    second entry chapter and one more line in the script, where raising the
    reserve would turn address space into resident memory on a two-core box. "One
    process per short horizon" now has a number attached to it.

C7. THE TRANSPILER'S BUMP HEAP IS A CEILING ON HOW MUCH ONE UNIT CAN CARRY.

    Mountains' four frames are fifteen polygons of up to 683 points, which is
    16,868 coordinates. As a Codex list literal that is a 139 KB gold and a 187 KB
    unit, and codexzig dies transpiling it:

        thread panic: cx heap: exhausted at 4294926367 + 614587 of 4294967296

    Not a slow build -- an abort, from the same never-reclaiming bump region C6
    describes, hit by the TRANSPILER on its own input rather than by a transpiled
    program. Tree's 628 coordinates and a 43 KB unit are nowhere near it; the
    ceiling sits somewhere between.

    THIS IS NOT THE ONLY WAY A BIG UNIT DIES -- see C14, where codexzig aborts
    with plenty of deck left, on a different mechanism. Watching the CX-DECK lines
    predicts this failure and not that one.

    The fix that keeps the grading honest is to stride the COORDINATES and keep
    everything else: point counts stay exact, so a silhouette that gained or lost
    a column is still a hard failure, and what thins is only the sampling density
    of a curve that is smooth by construction. Every eighth point took the unit
    from 187 KB to 34 KB. Prefer that over dropping a frame -- the frames are the
    BRANCHES, and branch coverage is worth more than coordinate density.

C8. A PER-FRAME PURE PROGRAM NEEDS AN ARENA RESET, AND THE SHIM IS THE PLACE.

    C6 says the bump heap never reclaims. In a browser that is not a slow leak, it
    is an immediate death: a blitter calls renderFrame() sixty times a second, and
    the heap is gone in under a second. Measured across the history, every build
    of the proof of concept:

        the rail-era build (7008fce)      42 frames    0.7s at 60fps
        the sky-era build  (9b6d6a5)      42 frames    0.7s
        with the real mountain ranges      1 frame     immediately
        with the arena reset          200,001 frames   55 minutes, still exact

    THE PAGE HAD NEVER WORKED FOR MORE THAN A SECOND, and nobody knew, because
    blitter.js renders up to thirty warm-up frames behind a spinner before it
    reveals the canvas -- so the failure looked like a spinner and then a dead
    canvas, which reads as a build problem rather than a heap one. It cost real
    eye-testing time and was found only by accident, while adding a scrub control.

    THE GATE COULD NOT SEE IT: harness/build_wasm.sh decoded exactly ONE frame and
    passed every one of those builds. That is the lesson worth more than the fix --
    a gate must exercise the shape the PRODUCT uses, not the smallest shape that
    proves the wire is connected. It now advances and renders 1200 frames.

    A bigger reserve buys frames and does not fix the shape. The fix is that the
    frame is PURE: nothing computed in it outlives the call, because the shim
    copies every coordinate into its own buffer before returning and the Codex
    side has no mutable state to carry over. So the region can be rewound to where
    it started and handed to the next frame:

        var cx_hp_base: i64 = 0;
        if (cx_hp_base == 0) cx_hp_base = cx_hp else cx_hp = cx_hp_base;

    Capture the base rather than writing 6291456, which is bare metal's own boot
    value for the heap pointer and mirrored by the plug -- see the deck note.

    THE SHIM IS THE ONLY PLACE THAT KNOWS A FRAME HAS ENDED. Nothing inside the
    transpiled program can know it; that is exactly the knowledge an arena needs,
    and it is why this belongs at the boundary rather than upstream.

C9. RELEASEFAST WAS THE ENTIRE COST OF THE SWEEP AND BOUGHT NOTHING

    Per module the loop was flat at about 50 s no matter how big the port was --
    Pond's 495 lines of generated zig cost the same as Render's 1,714. All of it
    was `zig build-exe -O ReleaseFast`, run TWICE per module (probe and check):

        pond.zig, 495 lines   ReleaseFast 22 s     Debug 1 s
        transpile                          2 s
        the graded program itself running  0.5 s

    So an eleven-module sweep was nine minutes, of which about eight and a half
    were ReleaseFast. README's "about five seconds per module" was never right.

    I FIRST BLAMED THE CODEX RUNTIME PRELUDE. Wrong, and the disproof was sitting
    in the sentence above: a cost that is the same for 495 lines and for 1,714 is
    a FIXED cost, which is not what a prelude-proportional one looks like. A
    two-line program whose entire body is one std.debug.print costs the same 23 s:

        hello, 2 lines, one std.debug.print   Debug 0.94 s   ReleaseFast 23.5 s
        build/pond.zig, 495 lines             Debug 0.85 s   ReleaseFast 23.8 s

    It is LLVM compiling ZIG'S OWN formatting machinery, on a two-core box. Our
    code is nearly free by comparison -- 1,200 extra lines of generated Codex cost
    about 0.05 s. (An empty `pub fn main() void {}` builds in 0.24 s and proves
    nothing: with no std reachable there is nothing to optimise.)

    THAT MATTERS BECAUSE IT KILLS THE TEMPTING FIX. The generated files really do
    share a byte-identical prelude, so precompiling it once as a module and
    linking looks like the obvious win. It would save under half a second across
    the whole sweep, and it would cost the transpiler a module boundary it does
    not currently emit. The fixed floor is zig's std, and no amount of prelude
    surgery touches it.

    AND `zig build-exe` NEVER CACHES. Rebuilding an UNCHANGED probe a second time
    took 28 s against the cold build's 22 s -- slower, not cached. It writes no
    .zig-cache in the cwd, an explicit --cache-dir is written to and never read
    back, and it does not skip when the output binary is newer than the source.
    So this was not a one-time cost being amortised; it was 25 s per module on
    every single sweep, forever.

    `zig build` -- the build SYSTEM, with a build.zig -- caches completely: 24.5 s
    cold, then 0.04 s unchanged, and 0.04 s after a touch that does not change
    content. That is the escape hatch if ReleaseFast is ever wanted back here.
    `-fno-llvm` also takes ReleaseFast to under a second, but it is a different
    codegen, so by this note's own argument it is no more "the shipping
    arithmetic" than Debug is.

    THE FIRST ATTEMPT AT THIS NOTE CLAIMED THE LEVEL CHANGES NO ANSWER. That was
    wrong, and how it was wrong is the interesting part. It moves exactly one
    value in the whole port:

        sky's sun_x    ReleaseFast -6.9129944     Debug -6.913025

    about 36 ulps, or 4.4e-6 relative -- thirty times the f32 width gap. One value
    out of 13,637, and it is in the seam D5 built its floor for: a sun height that
    is 244 minus a term of nearly the same size, carrying 38,180x cancellation.
    The optimiser reassociates, and the only place that survives to a visible
    difference is the one place designed to amplify it. Everything else in the
    port is bitwise identical between the two levels.

    THE FIX IS NOT TO CHASE THE "RIGHT" LEVEL, BECAUSE THERE ISN'T ONE. The game
    ships TWICE, at two levels on two targets -- the browser wasm at -O
    ReleaseSmall (ops/build_safari_wasm), the native screensaver at ReleaseFast
    (games/driving/build.zig:15). A native ReleaseFast probe is not "the shipping
    arithmetic"; it matches neither shipped binary authoritatively. The gold is a
    REFERENCE computation of the game's algorithm, and what makes the port
    trustworthy is the calibrated tolerance, not the last bit of one build.

    So: Debug for both, and the sweep went 9m06s -> 38s cold, still GREEN on all
    107 seams. angry-gopher splits it the same way -- shipping builds optimised,
    every check a bare `zig test`.

    AND THE 36-ULP SPREAD IS A RESULT, NOT A NUISANCE. It measures how far the
    oracle itself is allowed to wobble between honest builds of the same source,
    and D5's gate already admits it with room to spare. A port graded tightly
    enough to fail on a build-mode change would be a port measuring the compiler.

C10. THE SWEEP SKIPS WHAT HAS NOT CHANGED, AND THE KEY IS THE BUNDLED UNIT

    Debug took the sweep to 29 s, and every second of that was still being paid
    on runs where nothing had changed. Two stamp files per module in build/ take
    an unchanged sweep to 2.9 s, with byte-identical output.

    THE CHECK'S KEY IS THE BUNDLED UNIT, and that is the whole trick. The unit IS
    the transitive closure of every `cites` edge -- the port chapters, Grade, the
    gold just written, and the foreword chapters that live outside this repo -- so
    hashing it covers all of them with no dependency list to keep in step.
    Verified: perturbing Pond's duck-height rebuilt Pond AND Render, because
    Render cites Pond, and nothing else. Bundling is 50 ms, so it runs every time
    and only the two slow steps are skipped.

    THE GOLD'S OWN HASH IS PART OF ITS KEY. That is what keeps README's promise
    that a stale or hand-edited gold cannot survive one run. Verified by injecting
    a bogus 999.0 into gold/PondGold.codex: the next sweep regenerated it from the
    zig and went green. Leave the gold out of its own key and this optimisation
    would quietly turn a generated file into an editable one -- which is exactly
    the "second copy of the oracle" failure the whole harness exists to prevent.

    The graded programs still RUN every time, all eleven of them, for 1.9 s total.
    Skipping those would save little and would make "a check that printed nothing
    is a check that did not run" a lie.

    Measured: 28.9 s cold, 2.9 s unchanged, 7.2 s after a one-chapter edit.

    NOT DONE, MEASURED AND REJECTED: running the modules in parallel. `xargs -P2`
    gave 20.9 s against 30.5 s serial and `-P4` gave nothing more -- the box has
    two cores -- for interleaved output and a harder failure check. The stamp skip
    subsumes it on the common case.

C11. AN SVG WITH AN XML ERROR IS NOT A BROKEN IMAGE, IT IS NO IMAGE

    The spike loop's speed graph did not render for two rounds, and everything
    that could be checked from the outside said it was fine: the file existed, it
    served with a 200 and the right byte count, its polylines were entirely
    on-canvas, and the index referenced it. Steve hard-reloaded and still saw
    nothing, which is what ruled out the cache and left the file itself.

    ONE EM DASH. The graph's caption used `&mdash;`, which is an HTML entity. SVG
    is XML, and XML predefines only &amp; &lt; &gt; &quot; and &apos; -- everything
    else is an UNDEFINED ENTITY, which is a fatal parse error. A browser handed an
    SVG that does not parse renders nothing at all: no console error visible from
    outside, no broken-image icon, just empty space where the picture should be.
    The scene stills were unaffected because they are pure polygons and carry no
    text.

    Use numeric references (&#8212;) or plain ASCII in generated SVG.

    THE REAL LESSON IS THE GATE, NOT THE ENTITY. Every check available from
    outside the file passed, so the loop that was built to shorten feedback
    silently produced nothing twice. harness/spike_svg.py now parses each SVG with
    ElementTree BEFORE writing it and refuses to write one that is not well-formed
    -- free to check here, expensive to diagnose from a browser. Same shape as the
    build_wasm gate that renders a thousand frames rather than one: a gate has to
    exercise the thing the consumer actually does with the artifact.

C13. THE WASM HEAP IS SIZED BY ONE FRAME'S HIGH-WATER, NOT BY A LEAK.

    Adding the animals' stills took the browser page from 2,132 draw commands to
    2,368 and it began dying at frame 202 with "unreachable". That reads like a
    leak and is not one: the arena is rewound every frame (C8), so what matters is
    the peak of a SINGLE frame, and drawing 2,368 commands through persistent
    lists costs far more transient memory than the 166 KB of wire it produces --
    every `&` concatenation copies.

    Measured by bisection: 48 MB dies, 96 MB survives 1,200 frames. Set to 256 MB,
    about three times the requirement. .bss costs nothing until the pages are
    touched, so reserving generously is nearly free, and wasm32 caps the whole
    address space at 4 GiB. build_wasm.sh takes HEAP_MB to re-measure.

C14. C7 SHAPES STRUCTURE, NOT JUST TOLERANCES

    C7 recorded the bump-heap ceiling as a reason to grade Mountains at every
    eighth point. It also decides how CHECKS ARE DIVIDED, which is a bigger
    consequence and took two failures to see.

    The two generated still tables are 216 KB (EmojiStills) and 534 KB
    (CatStills). Bundled into RenderCheck on top of what it already cites,
    codexzig ABORTS -- a core dump, not a diagnostic. Bundled together into one
    StillsCheck it still aborts. Each alone transpiles comfortably.

    AND IT IS NOT THE DECK. It died with 68 MB used of 512 MB reserved, so the
    reported arena had ample headroom; the abort is somewhere else in the
    transpiler. Watching CX-DECK is therefore not enough to predict this.

    So: ONE CHECK PER GENERATED TABLE. judge/CritterCheck grades the animals,
    judge/CatDrawCheck grades the cat, and RenderCheck went back to grading the
    collection. That is three checks where the module structure would have
    suggested one, and the reason is entirely the toolchain.

    The practical rule: a generated data chapter is a bundle-sized thing, and the
    check that cites it should cite almost nothing else.

C15. A NEW MODULE MUST NOT BE ABLE TO KILL THE SWEEP SILENTLY.

    The stamp skip added in C10 keys the gold on `cat probe/... gold/<X>Gold.codex
    | sha256sum`. A NEW module has no gold yet, so `cat` exits non-zero, `pipefail`
    propagates it, and `set -e` ended the whole run -- printing NOTHING, because
    the stderr was already redirected to /dev/null to hide the missing-file
    message.

    Adding a module is the most ordinary thing this repo does, and the failure
    looked like the harness silently doing nothing. `|| true` inside the group is
    the fix: a missing gold simply means the key cannot match, which is exactly
    the "regenerate it" answer wanted.

    The general shape is worth keeping: an optimisation that assumes steady state
    has to be checked against the FIRST run, not just the second.

C16. THE THIRD ARM: THE SAME CHECK ON BARE METAL, AND WHAT IT COST TO GET.

    ./harness/metal.py <Chapter>...   or --all

    The port is verified against the game through the ZIG PLUG: Codex in, zig
    out, a native binary that prints a verdict. That proves the port and says
    nothing about the plug. metal.py runs the SAME check a second way -- through
    the Codex compiler's own x86-64 emitter, as a kernel image booted under QEMU
    -- and requires the two to print the same bytes. Diverse Double-Compiling in
    Wheeler's sense, on a program rather than on the compiler.

    IT WAS ABOUT FORTY LINES OF HARNESS, and that is worth recording because it
    had been imagined as a project. The machinery is the ladder's and is borrowed
    rather than restated: `ring_compile.compile_ring` streams a cite-resolved unit
    into the seed under QEMU and hands back a .cdx; `codex_vm.run_cdx` boots that
    and captures the serial. Both take the compute lock and both refuse off the
    venue, so this cannot start a guest without asking. The blob framing is
    `b"CDX map\n" + unit + b"\x04"`, which bare_expected.py in the ladder is the
    model for.

    IT IS FASTER THAN EXPECTED: 2m53s for all fifteen checks, two guests each.
    Pond (17 KB unit) is 8 seconds; Safari (1.16 MB, over the ring window, so it
    exercises the refill path) is 40. The units are far bigger than anything the
    ladder's own corpus compiles and nothing complained.

    ALL FIFTEEN AGREED, FIRST RUN, BYTE FOR BYTE -- including the whole-frame
    check's 3,091 commands and 7,518 coordinates. No tolerance is involved: this
    compares the two arms' PRINTED OUTPUT, and the verdicts are text.

    WHAT IT FOUND WAS IN THE DIAGNOSTICS, NOT THE OUTPUT. The seed emitted 1,268
    CDX4010 (bounds proven, info), 15 CDX4030 (pipeline, info) and TEN CDX3006
    warnings -- three real name collisions in a port that had been green for its
    whole life: `bar-quad` in both Tower and GuardRail, `tower-beyond` and
    `tower-right` in both Render and RenderCheck. codexzig's diagnostic stream
    carried none of them. All three are renamed; the missing diagnostics are
    FINDINGS.md item 5.

    A CHECK COMPARES VERDICTS, NOT VALUES, and the second mode is the answer.
    `Grade` prints "name ok 2468", so two arms that both sit inside a tolerance
    agree whatever their last bits did. `metal.py --entry <PocMain>` runs an
    ENTRY chapter instead, and the spike entries print every coordinate as a
    scaled integer: SpikeTruckMain is 89,902 exact values over two frames -- sky,
    ground, trees, towers, rails, elephants and the whole truck -- and the two
    arms agree digit for digit. That is the statement worth making. Verdict
    agreement is the cheap sweep.

    THE GUEST'S HEAP IS THE CEILING ON WHICH ENTRIES FIT, and it is lower than
    the host's 4 GiB. Measured: SpikeMain's six viewpoints die after ONE frame at
    run_cdx's default 1 GB, and after FIVE at the venue's 3072 MB, printing
    `OUT OF MEMORY` with HEAP at 2.94 GB -- the guest saying so cleanly rather
    than corrupting. C6 again, with a smaller budget, and the existing
    SpikeMain/SpikeTruckMain split is already the shape of the fix.

    A failed --entry costs a full idle timeout (five minutes of silence) because
    the guest goes quiet rather than closing the port. Six minutes to be told
    OUT OF MEMORY is not a hang.

    THE LESSON IS ABOUT ARMS, NOT ABOUT THIS PORT: an arm you develop against is
    an arm whose silence you have learned to trust. B5 records the flat-namespace
    hazard as something to watch for by hand, and by hand is how it was watched
    for, and three instances still got in. A second compiler is a second opinion
    about your own source, and it is cheap.

================================================================================
D. NUMERIC CALIBRATION -- what the tolerances are actually measuring
================================================================================

    Every gate in judge/ traces to one of these.

D1. The f32/f64 gap is about 1.2e-7 relative, and that is the floor. The port
    computes in Real (f64); the game computes in f32; the gold reaches Codex as
    the shortest decimal that round-trips the f32, re-parsed at f64.

D2. Camera and Geom hold at 1e-6 RELATIVE over 375 values. Tightened to 1e-7,
    six of nine seams go red — so the gate sits one decade above the width gap
    and nothing more. It is calibrated, not slack.

D3. SCREEN COORDINATES NEED A MIXED GATE, atol + rtol*|want|. Both halves were
    measured, and each covers the other's failure:
      - relative fails at the horizon. A screen y is 300 minus a term of about
        the same size, so y = 20.6068 is a small DIFFERENCE OF TWO LARGE NUMBERS
        and cancellation amplifies the relative error by the 300/20.6 between
        them — 1.3e-6, about fifteen times f32 epsilon.
      - absolute fails at the near plane. A point 37 m to the side at 0.4 m
        forward projects to x = -62929, where ONE f32 ULP IS ALREADY 0.0075 px,
        seven times a thousandth-of-a-pixel floor.
    In use: atol 1e-3 px, rtol 1e-6. At a hundred pixels that is about a
    thousandth of a pixel, which is the precision worth asserting.

D4. Structure is graded EXACTLY and must stay that way. Tags, colours and point
    counts take no tolerance. A wrong point count is a structural error and no
    coordinate tolerance should ever be able to hide one.

D5. A TOLERANCE FLOOR IS CALIBRATED PER SEAM, not copied from another one.

    D3 set the screen-coordinate gate at atol 1e-3 + rtol 1e-6 and that is right
    for the tree, whose coordinates run to -62929 where one f32 ulp is already
    0.0075 px. Sky's heights are screen pixels too and take the same SHAPE of
    gate, for the same cancellation reason -- but copying the 1e-3 floor with it
    would have been slack, and measurably so.

    The cancellation here is the worst yet seen. A sun height is 244 minus a term
    of nearly the same size, so with the disc's centre on the horizon it is
    244 - 243.993609 = 0.006391, and the f32/f64 gap there is:

        absolute   2.6e-6 px           nothing
        relative   4.1e-4              four hundred times a 1e-6 relative gate
        amplified  244/0.0064 = 38180x  (the tree's horizon case was 15x)

    So the floor has to admit 2.6e-6 and nothing else on that seam comes near
    zero. 1e-4 admits it with forty times to spare; 1e-3 sits four hundred times
    above the gap it exists for, and that is not free -- MEASURED: at 1e-3 a
    perturbation of sun-start-px by 2e-4 left sky-height GREEN while two other
    seams reddened. At 1e-4 the same perturbation reddens it.

    The rule: take the SHAPE of the gate from the failure mode, take the NUMBER
    from what that seam actually has to admit. A floor inherited from a seam with
    different magnitudes is a floor nobody calibrated.

D6. THE CHAIN IS THE THIRD DISTINCT CANCELLATION, and the first in METRES.

    D3 and D5 both amplified at the screen: a coordinate near the horizon, a sun
    height near zero. Render's `at` does it in world space and for a different
    reason -- not because the answer is near zero, but because the COMPOSITION
    passes through coordinates a thousand times larger than the answer.

    Measured, at the rider's second chain segment, 160 m past its end:

        composed bottom-left frame   a = 1235.67 m, x = 56.91 m
        rider frame                  right = -1.5169 m
        right is                     -1115.7*sin(yaw) + 54.3*cos(yaw)
                                     two ~55 m terms that nearly cancel
        amplification                1235.7 / 1.52 = 815x
        absolute error               2.9e-5 m   (29 microns, a km away)
        relative error               1.9e-5     (20x every other metre seam)

    SO THE ERROR TRACKS THE LARGEST COORDINATE THE COMPOSITION TOUCHES, not the
    size of the answer -- that is what f32 has to hold on the way through. Which
    makes the floor a representation granularity and not a fudge factor, and it
    is set as one: one f32 ulp at the range the seam reaches.

        r-at    reaches 2015 m; one ulp there is 1.22e-4. Floor 2.5e-4 (two ulp).
                Reddens at 1e-4, passes at 1.2e-4.
        r-prev  composes ONE join, reaches 660 m; one ulp there is 6.1e-5.
                Floor 6e-5 (one ulp). Reddens at 1e-5, passes at 2e-5.

    The two differ because the two reaches differ, which is D5 applied rather
    than restated. Giving the behind joint the chain's floor would have been four
    times slacker than anything it needs.

    AND THE PORT'S OWN MATH IS NOT THE CAUSE. Recomputing `at` in f64 with the
    language's own @sin/@cos and comparing to the real f32 render.at gives the
    same picture -- 3.9e-7 at d = 0, 1.9e-5 at d = 2 -- so this is the f32/f64
    width gap amplified by the composition, and Trig is innocent. Note also that
    the error does NOT grow with depth: it peaks at d = 1 and d = 2 and falls
    away by d = 6. It is cancellation at particular geometry, not accumulation,
    so a deeper chain is not a worse chain.

D7. THE CAT'S LEAP IS THE PORT'S ONE DELIBERATE APPROXIMATION, AND IT IS BOUNDED.

    cat.zig front-loads the airborne lerp with `pow(b, 0.7)`. It is the ONLY pow
    in the whole game -- the sole other transcendental outside the trig is
    rider.zig's `@exp`, which Num ports as exp-real -- so what was missing was a
    LOGARITHM, since pow is exp of a log, and the foreword has none.

    The port does not write one. 0.75 is DYADIC where 0.7 is not, so b^0.75 is
    `sqrt(b * sqrt(b))` -- verified exact to 1.1e-16 -- using a square root both
    languages already have.

    THE COST IS MEASURED, NOT WAVED AT. |b^0.7 - b^0.75| peaks at 0.02537 near
    b = 0.252 and is ZERO at both ends, so the cat launches and lands in exactly
    the right places and only the arc between them bows. The leap spans
    mid-across 0.816 to end-across -3.490, which is 4.306 m, so nothing can be
    more than 0.1093 m out. CatDrawCheck splits the stream to gate it honestly:
    235 non-airborne samples at the ordinary width tolerance, 7 airborne ones at
    0.12, and the POSE INDEX exactly on all 242 -- an approximation to the arc
    must never be allowed to flip which still is drawn.

    THE GAME IS NOT EDITED FOR THE PORT'S CONVENIENCE (Steve). Changing cat.zig to
    b^0.75 would make this exact, and cat.zig is the oracle and the deployed
    screensaver, so it stays as it is and the PORT carries the difference.

    ln-real remains a reasonable thing to write one day -- a logarithm is a
    foreword gap like r-atan, and Num already has its other half. It would need
    its own accuracy calibration. One call site is not much reason on its own.

D8. A NEAR-PLANE CLIPPED VERTEX IS THE LOOSEST SCREEN GATE IN THE PORT, AND THE
    RATIO IS ARITHMETIC RATHER THAN A MYSTERY.

    TruckBodyCheck grades coordinates at 1.5e-5 relative where every other screen
    seam holds at 1e-6. One vertex sets it: a tire in the case where the truck is
    two metres ahead of the rider, so the trailer rear is BEHIND the eye and the
    face is clipped.

    A clipped vertex sits at forward = near = 0.4 m EXACTLY, by construction — the
    clip puts it there. So the projection divides by 0.4 and multiplies by the 685
    px focal: a 1,713x amplification of any error in metres, and the error in
    metres is one f32 ulp at the largest coordinate the rider transform passed
    through. In that case it is his own `along` of 100 m, where an ulp is 7.6e-6 m.
    7.6e-6 x 1713 = 0.013 px against an x of about -1300, which is 1e-5 relative.
    Measured: the stream reddens at 7.5e-6 and passes above it.

    THIS IS D6 IN A THIRD UNIT. D3 amplified by a small screen y, D6 by a long
    chain in metres; this one amplifies by the near plane. The common shape is
    that the gate must track the LARGEST coordinate a composition passes through,
    never the size of the answer. The floor is what changes; here it is not even
    load-bearing, because every coordinate in the case is hundreds of pixels.

    And it is the near plane's looseness, not the truck's: the same seam holds at
    1e-6 in every case where nothing is clipped. The cost of the tighter gate
    would be dropping the clipping case, which is the only structural assertion in
    the check about what happens when the chase closes.

D9. TWO DEPTHS CLOSER THAN AN f32 ULP HAVE NO ORDER, AND THE SORT MUST SAY SO.

    The one place in this port where a comparison is deliberately not the zig's.

    render.zig sorts its scenery by an f32 depth. The port sorts by a Real, which
    is f64 in every plug (A7). Two depths that differ by less than the f32 grid
    are THE SAME NUMBER in the game -- its sort sees a tie and keeps merge order,
    which is what stability means -- while the port can see a difference and will
    order them by it. That is not fidelity; it is the port inventing an order out
    of precision the oracle does not have.

    FOUND BY THE WHOLE-FRAME CHECK AND BY NOTHING ELSE. A rail post and a rail
    bar swapped places in a 3,091-command stream: one adjacent transposition,
    every other command identical, and no per-module check could see it because
    both modules were right. Measured by bisecting the slack: they still swap at
    2e-8 relative and stop swapping at 6e-8, so the gap is between a sixth and a
    half of an f32 ulp (1.19e-7).

    THE THRESHOLD IS HALF AN ULP and the bound is the argument for it: two
    DISTINCT f32s differ by a whole ulp, so half an ulp is the largest slack that
    cannot tie a pair the game holds apart. Bigger would start inventing ties;
    smaller does not close this one.

    AND A PROPERTY CHECK HAS TO USE THE SAME RULE. RenderCheck asserts the sort is
    non-increasing in depth by counting inversions with a strict `<`. Against a
    sort with slack, that counts every pair the rule deliberately left in merge
    order and reports them as defects -- which it promptly did, at index 0 of the
    first state. A property asserted at a finer resolution than the thing it is
    asserted about is not a stronger check, it is a wrong one.

D10. RETIRING THE TRIG STAND-IN MOVED NO NUMBER AT ALL, AND THAT IS A
    MEASUREMENT RATHER THAN LUCK.

    A1 recorded port/Trig.codex as retirable once real-to-int and real-from-int
    landed, and warned: "Trig's wrap subtracts in a bounded loop where dm-reduce
    divides and converts back through the integers, so the two disagree in the
    last bits, and four graded chapters cite Trig today. It is its own change with
    its own regrade."

    The regrade was clean -- every check green, not one tolerance touched -- and
    the reason is worth having, because "it passed" is not a reason.

    THE TWO REDUCTIONS ARE BIT-IDENTICAL OVER THE GAME'S RANGE. Sampled at every
    thousandth of a radian across |x| <= 40 rad, 80,001 points: ZERO differ, and
    the sines of their outputs are equal to the bit. For |x| <= pi both are the
    identity; beyond it, repeated subtraction of a double 2pi and one
    divide-truncate-multiply land on the same double, and Sterbenz is why. The
    polynomial and the quadrant fold were already character-identical, so the
    reduction was the only place a difference could have come from.

    So the caution in A1 was right to be cautious and the answer is that the two
    agree exactly here. The general shape: when a note says "expect the numbers to
    move", the change is not finished until you have MEASURED whether they did --
    a green sweep answers whether the gates still pass, not whether the values
    changed, and those are different questions.

    WHAT DID NOT RETIRE IS `wrap`, and it was never part of the stand-in. Both
    mountains.zig and sky.zig carry their own bounded-loop wrap to normalise a
    bearing, and both are ported through Trig's. Deleting it with the sine broke
    two chapters with "CDX3002 Undefined name: wrap" -- a useful reminder that a
    helper written for one caller acquires others, and the way to find them is to
    grep before deleting rather than after.

================================================================================
E. WHAT A MODULE'S SURFACE DOES TO ITS CHECK
================================================================================

    Seams are chosen by the zig, not by the porter.

E1. RENDER.ZIG EXPOSES FOUR NAMES, AND THAT DECIDES HOW RENDER CAN BE GRADED.

    Its whole public surface is `Chain`, `Pose`, `at`, and `frame` (plus the two
    cull counters). buildChain, MapKind, Mapper, mapPt, emitGround,
    emitGroundColor, emitPondGround, emitJointGround, pushLeg and emitJointRail
    are all private to the file. A probe imports the module; it cannot reach any
    of them.

    Every other module in this port had a pub function per seam, so "the probe
    calls the real thing" was free. Here it is not, and the check has to say
    which of its rows is backed by what. RenderCheck grades three strengths:

      a REAL ORACLE      r-at. render.at is pub; the probe calls it.
      THE DEFINITION     r-prev. mapPt's prev branch is private, but it is
                         geom.curToNext then geom.toRider and both ARE pub, so
                         the probe composes the two real functions in that order.
                         Every arithmetic step is oracled; the ORDER is not.
      TRANSCRIBED        r-chain. buildChain is private AND `at` never consults
                         the caps -- hand `at` a chain of any length and it
                         composes it -- so the chain's EXTENT is not observable
                         through the public surface at all. LOOK_AHEAD (7) and
                         MAX_CHAIN (8) are copied from render.zig's source. Those
                         two integers are the only value in this port that is
                         transcribed rather than measured.

    THE WAY OUT WAS ALREADY IN THE FILE AND WAS TAKEN. `render.cull_seg` IS pub,
    and frame() accumulates into it `n_cows + n_pigs` for every chain index at or
    beyond FARM_SEG_REACH -- counts WorldCheck already grades exactly. So one
    frame() call turns the chain's extent into a number the world's own graded
    data predicts, and RenderCheck's f-cull seam does exactly that across eight
    rider states. The state on segment 16 is the one that pins it: a three-long
    chain never reaches the farm cull's third index, so its tally is zero.

    So r-chain no longer stands alone, and the two transcribed integers are
    constrained by a real oracle even though `at` itself can never see them. The
    lesson generalises better than the fix: an instrumentation counter can be the
    only window into a module's private structure, and it is worth hunting for
    one before accepting a transcribed value.

E2. CHECK WHAT IS `pub` BEFORE CHOOSING A SEAM, not after writing the port.

    The generalisation of E1. This port's seams have so far been chosen from the
    zig's own structure -- guard_rail's collect-then-draw, truck's motion-versus-
    body -- and that worked because those splits were also visibility splits. It
    is not guaranteed. Read the pub markers first; they are the real contract,
    and they can make a natural seam ungradeable and an awkward one free.

E3. RENDER'S PRIVATE RAIL-PATH CAP IS NOWHERE NEAR LIVE. RAIL_PATH_CAP is 192
    points and the longest path any joint produced across eight rider states was
    27 — run-up 11, both legs into and out of the apex, run-out 10. So the port
    leaves the cap out rather than porting a truncation that cannot fire, and
    that is now a measurement instead of an assumption. Same for the collection
    caps: MAX_VIS_TREES is 640 against a peak render itself puts near 37 percent.

E4. PORTING AN ALGORITHM MEANS PORTING ITS ANSWER, NOT ITS COST MODEL.

    render.zig depth-sorts its scenery with an insertion sort, which is right for
    a mutable array of a few hundred items. Ported literally onto Codex lists it
    exhausted the whole 4 GiB bump heap and killed the check.

    The reason is that an insertion sort has nothing to shift. Rebuilding the tail
    past the insertion point allocates a list PER ELEMENT, so one insert is
    quadratic in allocations and the sort is cubic — about 700 items across eight
    frames, and the heap that never reclaims (C6) turns that from slow into fatal.

    A STABLE MERGE SORT GIVES THE IDENTICAL ORDER. Same comparison, same tie rule,
    so nothing observable changes; the cost falls to quadratic in copies, roughly
    a hundred megabytes. The port takes the merge and says so at the definition.

    The general shape: an in-place algorithm carried across to persistent lists can
    gain a whole complexity class, and on this arm that is a crash rather than a
    wait. Check the cost model of anything that mutates in place before porting it
    line for line — the ANSWER is what has to match.

E5. THE GAME'S COMMENTS GO STALE AND THE CODE IS THE ORACLE. Not a defect in
    anything, and worth stating once because this port reads comments closely and
    they are usually excellent.

    truck.zig says three times that the headlight wedges and the brake-light glow
    are DEFERRED — the file header calls the truck "a placeholder blue dot",
    drawBody's own header says the wedges "need alpha/gradient seam tags the
    polygon-only blitter doesn't have yet", and the brake block says the halo is
    "deferred (needs a gradient/alpha seam tag)". All three are stale: the code
    calls drawWedge and drawBrakeGlow and pushes tag 4 for both, and paint.zig has
    had the tag for as long as the bull has.

    A port written from the comments would have emitted sixteen faces where the
    game emits twenty-four commands. The check would have caught it — a missing
    wedge is a missing command and structure is graded exactly — but only after
    the fact. The rule is cheap: read a comment for INTENT and the code for
    BEHAVIOUR, and when they disagree, grade the code.

E6. THE STRONGEST ORACLE IN THE PROJECT WAS AVAILABLE ALL ALONG: render.frame.

    E1 works through what render.zig's four public names cost the check -- the
    chain and the prev mapper graded indirectly, the collection observable only
    through two cull tallies. Reading it now, the fourth name is `frame`, and
    `frame` is the entire picture: backdrop, ground, and every kind dispatched to
    its drawer in one depth order. A probe can call it and read the words it puts
    on paint's wire.

    It could not be USED until the last drawer was ported -- a frame with an
    unported truck body or a flattened bull compares two different wires -- so the
    check that grades it arrived with the last of them, and immediately paid for
    itself twice: it found the bull's flattened gradients as a tag mismatch, and
    then the depth-tie transposition of D9, which nothing else could have seen.

    THE THING IT GRADES THAT NOTHING ELSE DID IS THE SEQUENCE. Every drawer was
    already graded in isolation; what was not graded at all was the dispatch --
    which kind goes to which drawer, in what order, with which focal. That logic
    had lived in the browser poc, outside every check, for the whole project.

    The general shape: when a module's public surface looks thin, check whether
    one of the names on it is the WHOLE THING. An orchestrator that takes state
    and produces output is a better oracle than any of its parts, and it is worth
    revisiting the day its last dependency lands.
